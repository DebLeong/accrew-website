How Much Does Bookkeeping Cost in Hong Kong?

A transparent breakdown of what businesses actually pay — and what they actually get — across every approach to bookkeeping in Hong Kong.

In this guide

  2. Part-time bookkeeper
  3. Full-time in-house bookkeeper
  4. Outsourced bookkeeping service
  5. Side-by-side comparison
  6. How to decide

Most Hong Kong business owners know roughly what they're paying for bookkeeping. Fewer know the full cost — including employer contributions, software licenses, onboarding time, and the hidden cost of errors or missed deadlines. This guide covers all of it.

1. DIY / spreadsheets

Typical cost: HKD 0 in direct fees. HKD 2,000–15,000/month in opportunity cost.

For very early-stage businesses with fewer than 50 transactions a month and a founder who has some financial background, DIY bookkeeping in spreadsheets can work. It works right up until it doesn't.

The problems compound over time: by month 6, reconciliation errors are harder to trace. By month 12, the catch-up cost before your annual audit typically runs HKD 15,000–50,000. By the time you need investor-ready financials, the state of your books can directly affect your ability to raise.

The opportunity cost calculation is simple: if a founder's time is worth HKD 400/hour and they spend 15 hours a month on bookkeeping, that's HKD 6,000/month in direct opportunity cost — more expensive than most outsourced options.

Best for: Pre-revenue businesses with fewer than 30 transactions a month, where the founder has accounting experience and is genuinely available to do it properly.

2. Part-time bookkeeper

Typical cost: HKD 8,000–15,000/month.

A part-time bookkeeper (20–30 hours/week, usually HKD 60–120/hour for an experienced professional) is a popular option for businesses with 50–200 transactions a month. They typically handle bank reconciliation, invoicing, AR/AP, and basic monthly reports.

The limitation is availability and continuity. Part-time professionals often work for multiple clients simultaneously. When your close is due, they may be finishing someone else's. Cover during illness or annual leave usually means falling behind.

If the bookkeeper is employed part-time (rather than freelance), the employer is required to contribute MPF on all relevant income. If they are freelance, you should confirm their own compliance arrangements to avoid unexpected liability.

Hidden costs:

  • Software license (typically HKD 300–650/month for Xero, depending on plan)
  • MPF if employment-based (5% employer contribution, capped at HKD 1,500/month)
  • Your time to manage them (typically 2–4 hours/month)

Best for: Established SMEs with moderate transaction volumes that don't yet justify a full-time hire.

3. Full-time in-house bookkeeper

Typical cost: HKD 32,000–55,000/month all-in.

Here is the breakdown for a typical full-time bookkeeper/accountant in Hong Kong:

This does not include office space allocation, equipment, or the management time required. It also does not account for the 3-month probation period, during which productivity is typically lower, and the turnover risk — the average Hong Kong bookkeeper changes roles every 18–24 months.

Best for: Businesses with more than 500 transactions a month, multiple entities, or complex payroll where a dedicated person adds clear value. Generally not justified below HKD 100–120k MRR for a services business.

4. Outsourced bookkeeping service

Typical cost: HKD 2,000–20,000/month depending on scope and quality.

The range is wide because the market is fragmented. At the lower end (HKD 2,000–5,000), you'll typically get data entry services: someone entering transactions into Xero, minimal review, basic P&L at month-end. At the higher end (HKD 10,000–20,000), you get a professional managing your entire finance function with monthly reports, compliance, and advisory.

The key questions when evaluating an outsourced provider:

  • Who actually does the work? Some firms offshore the work to junior staff in other jurisdictions and have a Hong Kong-based account manager who reviews. This can work, but understand the model.
  • What does "monthly reporting" mean? A one-page summary is different from a management accounts pack with commentary and budget vs actuals.
  • Is compliance included? Profits tax return preparation, MPF administration, and employer returns are often add-ons at lower-priced providers.
  • Who is your point of contact? If it's a junior coordinator, escalation time during close will be slow.

Best for: Most growing businesses with 50–500 transactions/month that want a complete finance function without the headcount commitment.

How to decide

The right answer depends on three variables: your transaction volume, the complexity of your reporting needs, and your tolerance for management overhead.

Go in-house if: You have more than 600 transactions a month, multiple entities, or your business depends on having a finance professional available full-time (e.g., a company with daily cash flow management needs or a regulated business with reporting obligations to an authority).

Outsource if: You have fewer than 400 transactions a month, want clean books and monthly reporting without the HKD 35k/month hiring commitment, and prefer to redirect that overhead into the business rather than headcount.

Keep DIY if: You are genuinely pre-revenue, have fewer than 30 transactions a month, and you or a co-founder have accounting experience and the time to do it properly. But plan your exit from this stage — the catch-up cost if you let it go grows quickly.

One honest note: many businesses stay in-house longer than they should because "changing systems feels complex." It isn't. A well-run transition to an outsourced provider takes 2–4 weeks and requires very little of your time.
